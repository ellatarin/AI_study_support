## Findings

### Distortion

#### Major — The morbidity section changes the lecturer's causal framing of biliary obstruction. The source says bile-duct obstruction interferes with digestion; liver failure is discussed separately as a consequence of overwhelming secondary metastases colonising the liver. The structured version instead states that biliary obstruction leads to liver failure.

- **In the structured transcript:** Morbidity and Mortality in Cancer — “Luminal obstruction” bullet
- **In the source:** Morbidity and mortality discussion

> "obstruction of the bile ducts in the liver interferes with digestion"; later, with secondary metastases, "you can get things like liver failure".

**Suggested fix:** Separate digestive impairment from bile-duct obstruction from liver failure caused by extensive metastatic involvement.

#### Minor — The companion-animal section makes the proposed cause of feline injection-site sarcoma more definite and more specific than the source. The lecturer described a personal case and said it was “proposed” to be due to an adjuvant added with the injection. The structured version states that feline injection-site sarcomas are associated with vaccine adjuvants.

- **In the structured transcript:** Comparative Oncology and Tumour Incidence — “Companion animals” bullet
- **In the source:** Companion-animal incidence discussion

> "feline injection site sarcoma. This is from injections proposed to be due to the adjuvant that is also added with the injection."

**Suggested fix:** Preserve the lecturer's uncertainty and avoid adding “vaccine” unless independently sourced.

### Omission

#### Major — Important clinical-pathology limitations on diagnosing invasion and metastatic disease are omitted. The lecturer explains that a biopsy samples only a limited area; adjacent lymph nodes may be sampled in breast cancer, but the entire body is not sampled, so occult secondary disease can be missed. The source also identifies assessment of clear surgical margins and depth of invasion as routine histopathological considerations. These qualifications make the otherwise categorical invasion/metastasis framing clinically usable.

- **In the structured transcript:** Benign vs Malignant Tumours: Invasion and Metastasis; Case Study: Colorectal Cancer Progression
- **In the source:** Immediately after colorectal invasion example

> "you'll also hear about looking for clear margins around any kind of biopsy"; "you might sample the adjacent lymph node, but you wouldn't be sampling all over the body"; "it might be difficult to necessarily find all of the possible secondary tumors".

**Suggested fix:** Retain the sampling, margin, and occult-metastasis caveats alongside the statements on invasion as a diagnostic/prognostic feature.

#### Major — The structured version substantially narrows the lecturer's tumour-incidence framing. It omits that malignant mesenchymal tumours are extremely rare, tend to occur in adolescence/young adulthood, and can progress rapidly and be lethal. It also omits the geographic examples of liver cancer and nasopharyngeal carcinoma being prominent in Chinese populations, including the lecturer's note of a substantial infectious contribution to liver cancer. These are lost epidemiological examples and clinical context, not merely repetition.

- **In the structured transcript:** Comparative Oncology and Tumour Incidence, or a dedicated human tumour-incidence subsection before it
- **In the source:** Tumour incidence discussion, before companion-animal slide

> "malignant tumors... from mesenchymal lineage cell types... are extremely rare"; "usually occur in young adulthood, in adolescence and young adulthood"; "liver cancer and nasopharyngeal carcinomas are very big in Chinese populations. There's also a large infectious element to liver cancer."

**Suggested fix:** Restore the omitted age, rarity, clinical-behaviour, geographic, and infection-linked incidence context.

#### Minor — Several clinically relevant screening caveats and numbers are lost: patients with vague/non-screenable presentations commonly make three, four, or five visits before diagnosis; cervical mortality reduction was especially apparent in older women and not markedly changed in younger groups; and ongoing cervical-cancer deaths were linked to non-follow-up after screening or declining screening. #### Minor — The source's additional explanation of the limitations and value conditions of screening is omitted. ### Underexplained

#### Minor — The colorectal case study loses the diagnostic distinction between a grossly observed polyp and a histologically diagnosed adenoma. The lecturer explicitly corrects the initial label: “polyp” is the neutral gross term for a stalked bowel growth, whereas it can only be called an adenoma after histology confirms no invasion. The structured heading “Polyp / Adenoma” and description of a “benign, non-invasive” polyp collapse this reasoning.

- **In the structured transcript:** Case Study: Colorectal Cancer Progression — step 2
- **In the source:** Colorectal precursor-lesion discussion

> "it's probably more neutral to call this a polyp"; "this is not necessarily an adenoma until I understand the histology"; after finding no invasion, "you can diagnose this as an adenoma."

**Suggested fix:** Preserve the sequence from gross polyp, to histological assessment of invasion, to adenoma diagnosis.

#### Minor — The cervical-screening account removes explanatory detail about why the junction is biologically relevant. The source says the squamocolumnar junction can move when the cervix is under stress and that the acidic vaginal environment can drive metaplasia from columnar toward squamous epithelium. Replacing this with the general phrase “environmental stress can trigger metaplasia” strips out the mechanism and tissue context.

- **In the structured transcript:** Cervical Cancer Screening — paragraph on the squamocolumnar junction
- **In the source:** Cervical histology and HPV discussion

> "This junction can be quite movable when the cervix is under... stress"; "Because of the acidic environment of the vagina, you can often get... metaplasia"; "columnar epithelium transitioning into... a squamous cell type."

**Suggested fix:** Keep the acidic-vaginal-environment and mobile-junction explanation when introducing metaplasia and HPV susceptibility.
