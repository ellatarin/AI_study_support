## Genetic and Epigenetic Basis of Cancer

A core concept in cancer biology is the model where a normal cell acquires an initial mutation, followed by a second mutation, becoming slightly abnormal. It then adds another mutation, becoming increasingly abnormal on its journey towards malignancy. These mutations accumulate in stepwise changes over time, although we do not have a precise timeframe for how long this progression takes.

Going from a normal cell to a malignant one involves broad categories of gene changes:

1. **Genetic changes:** Base substitutions, base pair changes, small deletions, small insertions, whole chromosome losses, chromosomal translocations, aneuploidy, and polyploidy. These changes are visible through genome sequencing or examining chromosomal morphology.
2. **Epigenetic changes:** Changes such as promoter methylation, microRNA expression, or histone acetylation/deacetylation altering the transcriptional state within the cell. These are not visible by conventional genome sequencing and require specialised techniques to detect.
3. **Infectious and inflammatory triggers:** Certain tumours arise from infectious agents. Around seven human viruses precipitate cancer and can predispose tissues to chronic inflammatory events that promote tumorigenesis.

This model raises several key questions:
- When do these gene changes occur?
- Are they identical across all cell types?
- How many mutations does it take?
- When must they occur?
- Which cells are more susceptible than others?

## Cancer as a Failure of Normal Differentiation

Every individual executes an elaborate differentiation program starting from a single-cell zygote. Most lineage specification and expansive growth occur during embryogenesis in the womb, progressing from a totipotent single cell to distinct cell lineages, with further maturation occurring through infancy, adolescence, and adulthood. Every cell understands its program of differentiation.

Organs are composed of tissues, and tissues are composed of cells that constantly turn over and cooperate as a community to maintain function. For example, the skin coordinates barrier, immune, sensory, and thermoregulatory functions across multiple participating cell types, with an inherent program of repair and regeneration.

Cancer represents a failure of this program—a failure of an individual cell to understand its role within the wider tissue architecture. This breakdown of the three-dimensional growth program leads initially to an in situ tumour within a single space. Over time, cells within the tumour gain the capacity to invade surrounding tissue and metastasise throughout the body.

### Tissue Architecture and the Basement Membrane

Histologically, normal tissue exhibits precise organisation. In normal breast tissue, epithelial cells of a milk duct are uniform in shape and size, displaying apical-basal polarity with nuclei orientated towards the lumen, surrounded by a well-behaved stroma containing vessels and fibroblasts.

In malignancy, cells lose this structural organisation, become pleomorphic in shape and size, breach the basement membrane, and invade the stroma. The basement membrane is a specialised extracellular matrix upon which epithelial cells sit; it provides structural support and adherence signalling. In the intestine, for instance, a single-cell-thick epithelial layer sits on a continuous basement membrane extending along each villus. Invasion occurs when deranged epithelial cells penetrate past this basement membrane into the stroma.

### Cell Culture and in vitro Limitations

Tumours are often explanted for histological analysis or disaggregated into single cells for in vitro culture. However, primary cells taken directly from tissue generally fail to grow in plastic tissue culture dishes. The establishment of HeLa cells in the 1950s—derived without modern consent protocols—was surprising because primary cells rarely propagate in culture. Studying isolated cells in 2D culture does not capture the complete in vivo picture, including stromal interactions, prompting modern cancer biology to move towards three-dimensional culture systems.

## Benign vs Malignant Tumours: Invasion and Metastasis

Historically, surgeons classified tumours into two categories:

- **Movable tumours:** Generally well-contained within a distinct capsule, allowing them to be lifted out without spreading into adjacent tissue. These correspond to benign tumours.
- **Immovable tumours:** Firmly adherent to surrounding tissue due to malignant cells invading into the adjacent stroma.

Histological identification of invasion is the most critical diagnostic and prognostic factor for clinicians.

### Mechanisms and Routes of Metastasis

Most cancer-related deaths occur when tumours metastasise—forming secondary tumours at distant sites. Malignant primary tumours disseminate via three main routes:

1. **Lymphatics:** Tumour cells enter lymphatic vessels and colonise regional lymph nodes.
2. **Haematogenous circulation:** Tumour cells enter venous or arterial blood vessels to seed distant organs (e.g. colorectal cancer metastasising to the liver, or breast cancer to the brain).
3. **Transcoelomic spread:** Tumour cells spread across body cavities, such as across the pleural cavity or the peritoneal cavity (common in ovarian cancer).

Histologically, secondary tumours often retain morphological characteristics of the primary site, such as forming glandular structures characteristic of adenocarcinomas.

Benign tumours, by contrast, remain circumscribed:
- **Meningioma:** A benign, encapsulated brain tumour that displaces rather than invades neural tissue, though it can still be fatal due to intracranial pressure.
- **Leiomyoma (fibroid):** A benign smooth muscle tumour of the myometrium that remains clearly demarcated and distinct from surrounding normal muscle.
- **Leiomyosarcoma:** A rare malignant counterpart that invades directly into the myometrial wall, interleaving with normal muscle fibres and exhibiting partial dedifferentiation.

Although millions of tumour cells may be shed into circulation, successful metastasis is an inefficient process; many shed cells fail to survive in secondary microenvironments. Some invasive tumours, such as basal cell carcinomas and approximately 30% of breast tumours, rarely or never metastasise.

### Case Study: Colorectal Cancer Progression

Colorectal cancer provides a clear model of stepwise tumour progression from precursor lesions to invasive malignancy:

1. **Normal epithelium:** Smooth, mucus-secreting colonic mucosal layer.
2. **Polyp / Adenoma:** A benign, non-invasive epithelial projection extending into the colonic lumen on a stalk (shaped partly by intestinal peristalsis). Histologically, the muscularis mucosae remains intact, serving as a proxy for the basement membrane.
3. **Adenocarcinoma:** Malignant cells breach the muscularis mucosae and invade the submucosa and muscularis propria. These invading cells often attempt to form abnormal glandular structures within the muscle layer.

This histological coexistence of adenoma and adenocarcinoma within the same lesion demonstrates that malignant tumours arise progressively from benign precursors.

## Tumour Nomenclature and Classification

Tumour nomenclature generally follows standard conventions, with specific suffixes indicating the cell of origin and malignancy status:

| Classification | Origin / Type | Nomenclature Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Benign Epithelial/Mesenchymal** | Various tissues | Tissue of origin + *-oma* | Lipoma (fat), Leiomyoma (smooth muscle), Papilloma (skin), Adenoma (glandular) |
| **Malignant Epithelial** | Endoderm / Ectoderm | Tissue + *carcinoma* | Breast carcinoma, Colorectal adenocarcinoma |
| **Malignant Mesenchymal** | Mesoderm | Tissue + *sarcoma* | Osteosarcoma (bone), Leiomyosarcoma (smooth muscle) |
| **Liquid Haematopoietic** | Blood lineages | *Leukaemia* | Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia |
| **Solid Haematopoietic** | Lymph nodes | *Lymphoma* | Hodgkin / Non-Hodgkin lymphoma |
| **Neuroectodermal (Malignant)** | Nervous system | Deviates from suffix rules | Glioma, Glioblastoma, Neuroblastoma, Retinoblastoma |
| **Melanocytic (Malignant)** | Melanocytes | *Melanoma* (always malignant) | Malignant melanoma |

Certain benign tumours do not become malignant; for example, uterine leiomyomas almost never progress to leiomyosarcomas. However, benign tumours can cause severe morbidity or death through mechanical obstruction, mass effect in confined spaces (such as a meningioma in the skull), or unregulated hormone secretion (such as pituitary adenomas).

## Morbidity and Mortality in Cancer

Primary tumours cause approximately 10% of cancer deaths directly; the remaining 90% result from secondary metastatic disease. Tumours cause morbidity and mortality via several mechanisms:

- **Space-occupying lesions and pressure:** Intracranial pressure from brain tumours/meningiomas.
- **Luminal obstruction:** Airway blockage by lung carcinoma leading to accumulated secretions, recurrent pneumonia, sepsis, and respiratory failure; biliary obstruction leading to liver failure.
- **Bone erosion and marrow failure:** Skeletal destruction causing severe bone pain and pathological fractures; marrow replacement leading to pancytopenia, severe anaemia, and fatal infections.
- **Ulceration and bleeding:** Chronic blood loss from gastrointestinal lesions leading to severe anaemia.
- **Systemic metabolic effects:** Cancer cachexia (wasting syndrome) and aberrant tumour product secretion.

In the UK, epithelial cancers—specifically breast, prostate, lung, and bowel—account for over half of all malignant cases and represent major causes of cancer-related mortality.

## Comparative Oncology and Tumour Incidence

Tumour development is a universal feature of multicellular organisms with differentiation programs:

- **Companion animals:** Dogs frequently develop mammary, cutaneous, and connective tissue tumours. Cats predominantly develop non-Hodgkin lymphomas as well as feline injection-site sarcomas (associated with vaccine adjuvants).
- **Other taxa:** Birds, reptiles, and mammals share similar high incidences of digestive, haematopoietic, and cutaneous neoplasms. Reptiles exhibit an approximately fivefold higher rate of malignancy once a tumour arises compared to mammals.
- **Invertebrates:** Oysters, flies, clams, and crabs possess developmental differentiation programs and can develop neoplastic growths.

## Clinical Detection and Screening Strategies

Early clinical presentations often involve non-specific symptoms ("itchy, crusty, lumpy, or bloody"). Because many tumours are non-palpable and non-visible, screening programs aim to identify pre-malignant or early-stage lesions before invasion occurs.

### Cervical Cancer Screening

The cervix connects the uterine cavity to the vagina via the endocervical canal, presenting a junction between:
- **Endocervix:** Glandular columnar epithelium.
- **Ectocervix:** Protective stratified squamous epithelium.

At the squamocolumnar transformation zone, environmental stress can trigger metaplasia. Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection at this site can induce dysplasia—a pre-invasive, non-malignant precursor state characterized by enlarged nuclei and mitotic figures extending throughout the epithelial layers while the basement membrane remains intact. Cytological screening (Pap smear) and HPV testing detect these morphological abnormalities, leading to a substantial decline in cervical cancer mortality since the mid-20th century.

### Colorectal, Breast, and Prostate Screening

- **Colorectal screening:** Endoscopic screening allows direct visualisation and removal of pre-malignant polyps/adenomas, though it carries risks such as bowel perforation. Non-invasive screening commonly uses the Faecal Immunochemical Test (FIT) to detect occult blood, though bleeding typically indicates established, ulcerated lesions.
- **Breast screening:** Mammography detects early lesions but carries a high false-positive rate, with approximately one-third of screened abnormalities resulting in overtreatment.
- **Prostate screening:** Serum Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) testing has limited specificity and does not reliably distinguish indolent from aggressive disease, making population-wide screening controversial.
