## Findings

### Distortion

#### Critical — The structured version converts tentative geographic observations into established causal claims. It says gastric and liver cancers in East Asia are "driven by dietary factors and endemic infectious agents" and that migrant studies show the "dominance" of environment over genetics, with Japanese migrants' rates rising "to match the host nation." The lecturer instead posed these as open questions and said the migration result merely "suggests there's something environmental." This overstates causal certainty in a section explicitly intended to teach critical appraisal of epidemiology.

- **In the structured transcript:** Epidemiology and Geographic Variation
- **In the source:** Epidemiology and geographic-variation discussion

> "Why are they high in Asia?"; "we're going to think about different geographic variations of infections"; Japanese migrants "started to develop more breast cancer. So that suggests there's something environmental."

**Suggested fix:** Restore the lecturer's uncertainty: present the patterns and migrant observation as evidence consistent with environmental contributions, not proof of specified causes or environmental dominance.

#### Critical — The two-stage model is presented as a universal requirement: "Carcinogenesis requires both initiation ... and promotion." The transcript presents the Berenbaum mouse-skin experiment as evidence for the initiator/promoter concept and says some exposures act as promoters rather than initiators; it does not claim that every cancer necessarily requires both stages. This turns a model/example into an unsupported universal rule.

- **In the structured transcript:** Initiators vs Promoters: The Two-Stage Model, opening sentence
- **In the source:** Asbestos and Berenbaum hypothesis discussion

> "This was the first study that really gave rise to this idea. This is the Berenbaum hypothesis"; "Some of them we instead consider to be promoters rather than initiators."

**Suggested fix:** Limit the claim to the experimental two-stage model and to cancers/exposures for which an initiation-plus-promotion framework is being used.

### Underexplained

#### Major — The structured epidemiology section removes the lecturer's central cautions about confounding, statistical flexibility, and the limits of association. It consequently does not adequately support the stated learning objective of appraising evidence linking exposures with cancer. The source also uses smoking as strong but not absolute evidence because lung cancer occurs in non-smokers.

- **In the structured transcript:** Epidemiology and Geographic Variation; Population Epidemiology and Biobanks
- **In the source:** Opening of epidemiology discussion; methodologies—epidemiology

> "there are lots of cool things you can do with statistics to make your epidemiological study look in whatever way you want. There's lots of caveats. There's lots of confounders"; "you also get lung cancer in non-smokers, so it's not as clear cut."

**Suggested fix:** Retain the methodological caveats and explain that epidemiological associations require careful interpretation rather than simply listing correlations.

#### Major — The mutational-signatures section supplies highly specific canonical signatures and lesions not given in the transcript, while omitting the lecturer's main interpretive limitation: real cancer genomes commonly contain mixtures of signatures, so a signature provides an indication of possible exposure rather than proof of a single cause.

- **In the structured transcript:** Methodologies for Determining Carcinogenicity — High-Throughput Mutational Signatures
- **In the source:** High-throughput sequencing / mutational signatures discussion

> "you do see mixtures of lots of different signatures in cancer cells, but it enables us to have some idea."

**Suggested fix:** Add the mixture/attribution caveat and avoid presenting detailed signature mappings not contained in the lecture as though they were transcript-derived.

#### Minor — The summary changes the lecturer's qualified conclusion—"much cancer is environmental"—into the stronger statement that cancer is driven "heavily" by environmental and lifestyle factors. It also omits the concluding risk-benefit framing: carcinogenic risk must be balanced against benefits (for example, diagnostic X-rays), and exposure dose may be too low to be consequential for many agents.

- **In the structured transcript:** Summary
- **In the source:** Lecture conclusion

> "much cancer is environmental, including lifestyle-related factors"; "Ultimately, it's all about the risks versus the benefits"; "we're probably not exposed to enough of it in high enough doses to worry about it."

**Suggested fix:** Use the qualified conclusion and retain dose and benefit-risk context, which are key to applying carcinogenicity evidence responsibly.
