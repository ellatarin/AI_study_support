# Fidelity Assessment: `structured-transcript.md` vs `transcript.txt`

**Source:** Cancer Biology, Lecture 1 (of 6) — invasion, metastasis, nomenclature, screening
**Raw transcript:** ~8,000 words | **Structured version:** ~2,000 words | **Compression ratio:** ~4:1
**Assessed:** 31 August 2026

---

## Verdict

The structured version is a competent and largely faithful summary. Its coverage of the *taught spine* — differentiation failure, basement membrane, invasion, metastatic routes, colorectal progression, nomenclature — is good, and the nomenclature table is materially clearer than the spoken original.

The losses are not evenly distributed. They cluster in three places:

1. **Human tumour incidence** — an entire block of the lecture (~600 words) is missing, not compressed. This is the single largest gap.
2. **Epistemic and methodological framing** — the lecturer repeatedly explains *why* something is hard to see, hard to sample, or hard to know. Almost all of this is gone, which leaves several downstream statements in the structured version asserted without their supporting rationale.
3. **Screening cost-benefit logic** — the structured version keeps the conclusions (mammography over-treats, PSA is poor) but drops the framework that generates them.

There is one genuine conceptual **distortion** (polyp/adenoma) and one **factual softening** (PSA recommendation status).

**Findings:** 9 lost, 11 underexplained, 5 distorted/imprecise, 6 unsupported additions.

## A. Lost concepts

Material content in the transcript with no counterpart in the structured version.

### A1. Human tumour incidence — entire block missing ⚠️ *highest severity*

The transcript devotes a substantial passage to incidence and epidemiology before moving to comparative oncology. The structured version jumps straight to "Comparative Oncology and Tumour Incidence" (animals) and retains only one sentence about the UK big four. Lost:

- **Benign tumours are extremely common** — lipomas, fibroids, warts, moles: *"who doesn't have moles?"*. The lecturer uses this to make a base-rate argument: *"it actually takes a lot for a benign tumour to become malignant."* This is a load-bearing concept — it's the population-level counterpart to the multi-hit model taught at the start of the lecture — and it is entirely absent.
- **Sarcomas: rare, young, lethal.** Malignancies of mesenchymal lineage *"hardly occur"*, arise in adolescence and young adulthood, progress fast, are difficult to treat. This is the only age-distribution content in the lecture. Entirely absent.
- **Geographical variation.** Liver and nasopharyngeal carcinoma prevalence in Chinese populations, with *"a large infectious element to liver cancer."* Entirely absent — and it's the only place the ~7 oncogenic viruses from Section 2 are cashed out in a real epidemiological example.
- **Incidence vs mortality are different rankings.** *"lung cancer is still pretty difficult to treat, and accounts for most of the deaths."* The structured version flattens this into "represent major causes of cancer-related mortality," losing the point that lung is disproportionately lethal relative to its incidence.
- Ovarian and cervical cancer named as further common types.

### A2. Invasion as an obligatory step for metastasis

Transcript: it is rare for a tumour to go from non-invasive to widespread metastases, and where that appears to happen *"you've missed something in terms of the sampling"* — therefore invasion is *"probably an obligatory step"*. The structured version states invasion and metastasis as sequential facts but never states the obligatory-intermediate claim or the sampling caveat attached to apparent exceptions.

### A3. Clear margins and depth of invasion

Transcript introduces two standard histopathology concepts: checking for **clear margins** to confirm the whole tumour was excised, and assessing **how far** the tumour has invaded. Neither appears in the structured version. Depth of invasion is the basis of staging — this is a notable omission for a lecture that names invasion as the key prognostic factor.

### A4. Tumour cells within a lymphatic duct

Transcript gives a specific, practical-relevant histological observation: tumour cells seen inside a lymphatic vessel changes the diagnosis to metastatic. This is lymphovascular invasion — a discrete diagnostic sign the students are told they'll look for. The structured version lists lymphatics as a *route* but drops the observable sign.

### A5. Biopsy sampling limitations

Transcript: with breast cancer you might sample the adjacent lymph node but *"you wouldn't be sampling all over the body"*, so secondaries can be missed absent another physical sign. This recurs as a theme (also in A2, A3). Wholly absent.

### A6. Time-to-diagnosis in practice

Transcript: because presenting symptoms are vague, patients typically make *"three or four or five visits before a diagnosis is made"*, against an NHS goal of earlier and faster diagnosis. The structured version mentions non-specific symptoms but not the diagnostic delay or the health-system context — which is the reason screening is being discussed at all.

### A7. Cervical screening outcome detail

The structured version compresses the mortality data to "a substantial decline ... since the mid-20th century". Lost:
- The decline is concentrated in **older women**; rates among younger women changed comparatively little.
- Residual deaths occur largely in women who **declined screening or were not followed up** after an abnormal result.

This is the most analytically interesting part of the "success story" and it's the part that was cut.

### A8. The second set of open research questions

The transcript poses two question blocks. The first (when do gene changes occur, how many, which cells) is preserved. The second — what mutations drive benign→invasive, is it tissue-specific, and *what happens at the distant site that lets malignant cells survive there* — is lost. The third question is the metastatic-niche problem, distinct from anything else in the lecture.

### A9. Model-simplification caveat

Transcript, immediately after presenting the multi-hit cartoon: *"Simplicity can help frame our thinking, but it will also end up losing a lot of details."* A deliberate epistemic hedge on the central diagram of the lecture, dropped.

## C. Distortions and precision errors

### C1. Polyp vs. adenoma collapsed ⚠️ *conceptual error*

The structured version's table row reads **"Polyp / Adenoma: A benign, non-invasive epithelial projection"**, treating the terms as synonyms.

The transcript makes the opposite point, explicitly and self-correctingly. The lecturer labels a lesion "adenoma", then retracts: it is *"a little bit premature of me"* — you cannot call it an adenoma until you have the histology. **Polyp** is a neutral macroscopic descriptor (something growing on a stalk in the bowel); **adenoma** is a histological diagnosis of a benign, non-invasive lesion. Merging them destroys the distinction between what you can see grossly and what you can only diagnose down a microscope — which is the organising idea of the whole colorectal section.

### C2. The two-polyp comparison is lost, and with it its lesson

Transcript shows **two** polyps that are macroscopically indistinguishable — *"they both look like little cauliflowers"* — one of which proves on section to be a pure adenoma and the other to contain an invasive adenocarcinoma component. The structured version reduces this to "histological coexistence of adenoma and adenocarcinoma within the same lesion". The coexistence claim is defensible for the second polyp, but the actual teaching point — **gross appearance cannot discriminate benign from malignant** — is gone.

### C3. PSA status softened

- Transcript: PSA is *"currently not recommended for the general populace"* — a definite statement of policy.
- Structured: "making population-wide screening controversial."

"Controversial" is weaker and different in kind from "not recommended". This is a factual downgrade, not a compression.

### C4. Breast over-treatment denominator shifted

- Transcript: of those who go for screening and then on to further treatment, about a third receive unnecessary treatment.
- Structured: "approximately one-third of **screened abnormalities** resulting in overtreatment."

Different denominators. The structured phrasing implies a rate over all flagged abnormalities.

### C5. "Over half" vs "half"

Transcript: the big four account for *half* of malignant cases in the UK. Structured: "over half". Minor inflation of a stated figure.
