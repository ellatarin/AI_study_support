# Fidelity Assessment (second opinion): Lecture 3 structured transcript vs transcript

**Lecture:** Biology of Disease, pipeline Lecture 3 — *The Nature and Biology of Cancer* (2026-03-04). The lecturer calls it the first of six cancer lectures.
**Sources:** `2026-08-31-lecture-3-transcript.txt` (8,073 words, 468 sentences) and `2026-08-31-lecture-3-structured-transcript.md` (~1,900 words of prose, 138 lines).
**Assessed:** 31 August 2026, independently of the report in `2026-08-31-lecture-3-fidelity.md`.
**Locators:** `S{n}` is the *n*-th sentence of the transcript when split on sentence boundaries; `L{n}` is a line of the structured markdown.

---

## Verdict

The structured version is accurate in outline and materially clearer in two places than the lecture was — the nomenclature table and the metastatic-route list both improve on speech. Its spine is intact: differentiation failure, basement membrane, invasion, routes of spread, the colorectal progression, nomenclature, screening.

What it is not is a structured transcript. It is a summary of roughly a quarter the length, and the losses fall almost entirely on **mechanism and evidence** rather than on content words. The pattern is consistent enough to name: wherever the lecturer says *why* something is true, hard to see, or hard to know, the conclusion survives and the reasoning does not. That is the material a student needs in order to use a claim rather than recite it.

Two further patterns worth separating from volume:

- **Quantities drift.** Three numbers changed meaning in transit — a proportion, a denominator, and an invented magnitude. None is a rounding artefact; each reads as confident.
- **The text imports terminology and examples the lecturer never used.** Most are correct. One is wrong, and one is an inference presented as taught fact.

**Findings: 14 lost, 12 underexplained, 9 distorted, 8 unsourced additions.**

---

## A. Distorted — the structured version asserts what the source does not support

Highest risk: these read as authoritative and are wrong or unsupported, so they survive revision unchallenged.

### A1. Polyp and adenoma presented as synonyms — *critical*

**Structured (L81):** "**Polyp / Adenoma:** A benign, non-invasive epithelial projection…"

**Source:** the lecturer labels the lesion an adenoma and immediately retracts it — S226: *"it's a little bit premature of me to have done that because this is not necessarily an adenoma until I understand the histology of this particular polyp."* S227: *"it's probably more neutral to call this a polyp, and polyp simply means something that's growing on a stalk."* The name is only earned later, after invasion has been excluded — S251: *"you can diagnose this as an adenoma."*

Polyp is a macroscopic description; adenoma is a histological diagnosis. Merging them destroys the organising idea of the entire colorectal section — that what you can see grossly and what you can diagnose down a microscope are different things — and it does so by resolving a distinction the lecturer drew explicitly, on tape, against himself.

### A2. "Millions of tumour cells" — a magnitude the lecturer never gave

**Structured (L74):** "Although **millions** of tumour cells may be shed into circulation…"

**Source (S208):** *"significant numbers of them are released, but many of them never metastasize."*

The claim is plausible and may even be true. It is not in the lecture, and it is the kind of figure a student would repeat in an exam on the authority of these notes.

### A3. PSA policy status softened — *major*

**Structured (L138):** "…making population-wide screening **controversial**."

**Source (S462):** *"It is not a very, very good test, and it's **currently not recommended for the general populace**."*

A statement of current policy has become a statement of debate. These are different claims, and the second is weaker in a way that would change what a student writes.

### A4. Breast over-treatment denominator shifted

**Structured (L137):** "approximately one-third of **screened abnormalities** resulting in overtreatment."

**Source (S459–460):** *"many of the people that do go for screening **and then for further treatment**… about a third of those cases are unnecessary treatment."*

The lecturer's third is of those who proceed to treatment. The structured version's third is of flagged abnormalities — a larger denominator and a different, more alarming statistic.

### A5. "Over half" for "half"

**Structured (L112):** breast, prostate, lung and bowel "account for **over half** of all malignant cases."
**Source (S349):** *"they account for **half** of the malignant cases in the UK."*

### A6. The 90% complement stated as taught fact

**Structured (L104):** "Primary tumours cause approximately 10% of cancer deaths directly; **the remaining 90% result from secondary metastatic disease**."
**Source (S324–326):** the lecturer gives only the 10%, and attributes the rest to metastases qualitatively. The arithmetic is reasonable; presenting it as a taught figure is not.

### A7. Biliary obstruction attached to the wrong consequence

**Structured (L107):** "biliary obstruction leading to **liver failure**."
**Source (S312):** *"obstruction of the bile ducts in the liver **interferes with digestion**."* Liver failure appears elsewhere in the lecture (S327) and belongs to widespread metastatic disease, not to obstruction.

### A8. The lecturer's cat becomes feline epidemiology

**Structured (L118):** "Cats predominantly develop non-Hodgkin lymphomas **as well as feline injection-site sarcomas (associated with vaccine adjuvants)**."
**Source (S365–367):** non-Hodgkin lymphoma is the population claim; the injection-site sarcoma is *"my cat"*, and the mechanism is hedged — *"**proposed** to be due to the adjuvant"*. The structured version promotes an anecdote to an incidence statement and a hypothesis to an association. Dropping the anecdote is right; inheriting its certainty is not.

### A9. Endoscopy gains a therapeutic function

**Structured (L136):** "Endoscopic screening allows direct visualisation **and removal** of pre-malignant polyps."
**Source (S442–443):** *"you'd actually have to use an endoscope and go and look at those and **sample** those… you'd have to do the histology in order to be sure."* Removal is not claimed, and the requirement for histology — the actual point — is dropped.

## B. Lost — material content with no counterpart

### B1. The entire incidence discussion of benign tumours — *highest-volume loss*

**Source (S332–341).** Benign tumours are *"incredibly common"* — lipomas, fibroids, warts, moles (*"who doesn't have moles?"*) — and the lecturer draws the inference that matters: S335, *"it actually takes a lot for a benign tumour to become malignant."*

This is the population-level counterpart of the multi-hit model taught at the start of the lecture, and the structured version has none of it. Its "Comparative Oncology and Tumour Incidence" section covers animals only.

### B2. Sarcoma epidemiology — the lecture's only age distribution

**Source (S342–346):** malignancies of mesenchymal lineage are *"extremely rare… they hardly occur"*, arise *"in adolescence and young adulthood"*, are *"very difficult to treat"*, *"progress very quickly, and they're lethal."* Entirely absent.

### B3. Geographical variation, and the only place the oncogenic viruses are cashed out

**Source (S350–352):** liver and nasopharyngeal carcinoma *"very big in Chinese populations"*, with *"a large infectious element to liver cancer."* Also lost: ovarian and cervical cancer named as further common types. The seven oncogenic viruses from the opening section (S43) survive in the structured text as an abstraction with no epidemiological example attached.

### B4. Invasion as an obligatory step, and the sampling caveat that defends it

**Source (S212–213):** *"it's actually quite rare for a tumour to go from this state to having metastases all over the body, and that is probably you've missed something in terms of the sampling. So this is probably an **obligatory step**."*

The structured version states invasion and metastasis as sequential facts but never makes the claim that invasion is obligatory, nor explains why apparent counter-examples are sampling failures.

### B5. Clear margins and depth of invasion

**Source (S202–203):** *"looking for clear margins around any kind of biopsy, to see that you have lifted out the entire tumour"*; *"Histopathologists will look to see **how far** the tumour has invaded."* Both absent. Depth of invasion is the basis of staging, in a lecture that names invasion as the key prognostic factor.

### B6. Tumour cells inside a lymphatic duct as a diagnostic sign

**Source (S138–139):** seeing tumour cells within a lymphatic vessel changes the diagnosis to metastatic. The structured version lists lymphatics as a *route* (L63) but drops the observable sign the students are told they will look for in practicals.

### B7. The limits of biopsy sampling

**Source (S170):** with breast cancer *"you might sample the adjacent lymph node, but you wouldn't be sampling all over the body, so it might be difficult to necessarily find all of the possible secondary tumours unless there is some other physical sign."* Absent — and it recurs as a theme (see B4).

### B8. Diagnostic delay, and the reason screening is discussed at all

**Source (S384):** *"patients are usually making three or four or five visits before a diagnosis is made, even though it's the goal of the NHS to be able to diagnose earlier and quicker."* The structured version keeps the vague symptoms (L124) but not the delay or the health-system context that motivates the whole screening section.

### B9. The screening cost-benefit framework

**Source (S387–389):** before any case study the lecturer sets out the criteria — the cost of rolling it out across a population; catching the disease *"at a state at which it can be treatable"*; *"diminishing returns"* on treatment once malignant; and that the result must be *"useful for that patient"*. The structured version keeps every verdict that these criteria generate and none of the criteria.

### B10. Cervical screening outcomes — the analytically interesting half

**Source (S435–437):** the decline runs *"from the 1950s to… 2000"*, *"particularly amongst older women"*; *"This hasn't changed much for the younger populations"*; and residual deaths are in women *"that either are not followed up after their screen or that have declined screening altogether."*

**Structured (L132)** compresses all of this to "a substantial decline in cervical cancer mortality since the mid-20th century."

### B11. The second set of open research questions

**Source (S214–217):** what mutations take a tumour from benign to invasive; whether they are tissue-specific; and *"what happens in those distant sites that allow those malignant cells to actually survive?"* The first question block (S46–50) is preserved at L26–30; this one is gone, including the metastatic-niche problem, which appears nowhere else.

### B12. The model-simplification caveat

**Source (S30), immediately after the multi-hit cartoon:** *"Simplicity can help frame our thinking, but it will also end up losing a lot of details."* A deliberate hedge on the central diagram of the lecture.

### B13. The two-polyp comparison, and its teaching point

**Source (S256–259):** two polyps, macroscopically identical — *"They both look like little cauliflowers"*, *"You wouldn't necessarily be able to say anything… about either of these"* — one proving to be a pure adenoma, the other carrying an invasive adenocarcinoma component.

**Structured (L84)** reduces this to "histological coexistence of adenoma and adenocarcinoma within the same lesion". That is defensible for the second polyp and loses the actual lesson: gross appearance cannot discriminate benign from malignant.

### B14. The biopsy workflow

**Source (S124):** presentation with a lump → biopsy → assessment of whether cells are contained or invading. The structured version never mentions biopsy outside the screening section.

| C4 | **The normal squamous baseline** (L132) | S412–415: squames flattened, nuclei tiny, and *"you only ever get mitotic cells here at this basement layer."* The structured version describes only dysplasia, so "mitotic figures extending throughout the epithelial layers" has no stated norm to be abnormal against. |

## E. Non-findings — correctly dropped

Opening admin (*"shall we get started?"*), the forgotten clock (S109), slide-navigation fumbles (S89, S359–362), laughter and applause markers, the aside about pronouncing "metastases" (S132), the closing housekeeping, and the discourse markers *"Right?"* / *"Okay?"* throughout. The cat anecdote is rightly reduced to its substance, though see A8 for what came with it.

One judgement call recorded rather than raised: the lecture's closing thesis — *"you can really consider cancer as a disease of development and differentiation"* (S376) — is dropped, while the same idea survives in the structured version's opening section (L38). Losing the bookend is a rhetorical loss, not a conceptual one.

## Priority for reinstatement

1. **A1** — polyp/adenoma. A conceptual error, examinable, and it inverts something the lecturer said explicitly.
2. **B1, B2, B3** — the human incidence block: benign base rates, sarcoma age distribution, geographical variation. The largest volume of lost teaching, and the only place several of these ideas appear.
3. **A2, A3, A4, A5, A6** — the five quantitative and policy drifts. Cheap to fix, high cost if repeated in an exam.
4. **B9 with C11** — the screening criteria and what a positive FIT implies. The retained verdicts depend on both.
5. **C3, C4, C5** — the cervical histology chain, which currently defines dysplasia against a baseline it never states.
6. **B4, B5, B6, B7** — the invasion-and-sampling cluster: obligatory step, margins, depth, lymphovascular sign, sampling limits. All practical-facing, and the practicals are referenced throughout.
7. **B10** — cervical screening outcomes by age and by non-attendance.
8. **A7, A8, A9** — the three smaller distortions.
